Alberta AI Data Centres — Interactive Size & Impact Map

Alberta's grid operator (AESO) had 42 large-load connection applications on file as of Feb. 2026, requesting 21.1 GW combined — most of them AI data centres. This map names and locates 20 of them, including Meta's $13B first-in-Canada campus in Sturgeon County, Tourmaline's $15–20B Malachite One near Edson, and a new Indigenous-majority-owned proposal in Northern Sunrise County, collectively requesting thousands of megawatts of power and billions of litres of water annually. The rest are listed in AESO's queue only by project code and megawatt figure, with no developer, site, or footprint public yet — see the FAQ for why they aren't drawn here. This map lets you explore each of the 20 at real scale — overlaid on Alberta cities and towns — and compare land footprint, electricity demand, water draw, noise, and air-quality impact side by side. All figures are drawn from company disclosures, AUC filings, Alberta Major Projects, and municipal planning documents.

The Noise, Water, and Electricity layers are estimated projections based on publicly available research and industry benchmarks. They are for informational and educational purposes only and do not constitute professional engineering, environmental, or regulatory assessments.

Methodology

  • Noise: Zones based on typical data centre noise of 70–96 dB at source, free-field decay of ~6 dB per doubling of distance. Infrasound (below 20 Hz) reported beyond audible range. WHO residential limit: 45 dB day / 40 dB night.
  • 🔊 "What it might sound like" demo: The audio you can play from each noise zone is a synthesized illustration — not a real recording, measurement, or prediction for any specific site. It's built to give a rough sense of how low-frequency hum and broadband mechanical/cooling noise can change with distance (quieter, more muffled, shifting toward infrasound as you move farther away), based on the general dB ranges and decay assumptions described above. Treat it as a feel-for-scale aid only, not as evidence of what any particular community would actually hear.
  • Water: Uses Wonder Valley's publicly stated approved permit of 6 million m³/year (Smoky River). Preliminary AUC certificate: up to 24 million m³/year. City consumption calculated at 250 L/person/day (Statistics Canada average).
  • Electricity: Based on published GW capacity × 8,760 hrs/year. City consumption estimated at 15,000 kWh/person/year (Stats Canada 2022). Alberta total: ~100 TWh/year (AESO 2024).
  • Jobs (long-term): Permanent/operational job figures are company- and government-stated estimates from project filings, news coverage, and municipal releases (figures vary by source and project phase — treated here as rough order-of-magnitude only). Construction-phase jobs are temporary and shown for context. City workforce estimated at ~50% of population (Stats Canada labour force participation average). For the 5 sites added June 2026 (Wild Rose Power Hub, AHI Hub of Innovation, eStruxture CAL-3, Beacon Foothills AI Hub, Clive Power & Data Centre), published job figures were not available for all — figures shown are rough estimates scaled from comparable proposals of similar MW/footprint, for illustrative comparison only. For Malachite One (added August 2026), the company has given ranges rather than single figures (construction: "several hundred" short-term; operations: 300–500 data-centre jobs plus 40–60 power-plant jobs) — this map shows the midpoint of those ranges for chart purposes only; see the proposal's own note for the actual disclosed range.
  • 💰 Economic benefit: Capital-investment figures ($70B, $24B est., $12B, $4B, $10B, $13B, up to $20B est.) are company- and government-stated amounts from public project announcements and news coverage — see sources below. The "rough est. annual tax revenue" figure is NOT an official projection for any project; it applies a generic, commonly cited industry rule of thumb (roughly $3–6M in annual local/property tax revenue per $1B invested) to each proposal's stated investment, purely to illustrate order of magnitude — except where a project has published its own municipal tax estimate (e.g. Malachite One's own $52.4M/yr-by-2032 property-tax estimate to Yellowhead County), which is shown instead and noted as company-stated. Actual revenue depends on assessment methods, incentive agreements, and revenue-sharing arrangements not modelled here.
  • 💨 Air quality / emissions: "Concern level" reflects how much has been independently reported or established through regulatory review for that SPECIFIC proposal — it is not a measured pollution reading and not a ranking of which project is "worse." Wonder Valley's figures come from Canada's National Observer's reporting of an estimated ~30 megatonnes CO₂e/yr at full build-out (depending on technology/mitigation) and CBC's confirmation that the project is exempt from a full provincial Environmental Impact Assessment. Synapse Olds' AUC application was rejected twice — most recently on August 17, 2026, when the AUC found the site "simply not suitable" and Synapse confirmed the project "will not proceed as proposed" (CBC, Global News). Meta's Sturgeon County campus is paired with the $4.6B, 932 MW (scalable to 1.8 GW) Greenlight Electricity Centre gas plant (Pembina/Kineticor/Morgan Stanley); Canada's National Observer reported analysis putting a comparably sized gas-fed data centre's emissions at roughly 3 megatonnes CO₂e/yr, about 10% of Alberta's current electricity-sector emissions. For Beacon AI Hub, no on-site generation method or emissions estimate has been publicly disclosed as of June 2026. The general health-effects context (NOₓ, PM2.5 from gas/diesel generation) draws on independent research from Inside Climate News and the Piedmont Environmental Council on data centres elsewhere — included for general industry context, not as a claim about any specific Alberta proposal.
  • ⚡ Provincial power policy (added Aug 2026): On June 9, 2026 Alberta enacted the Data Centre Regulation (AR 117/2026), codifying a "bring your own generation" (BYOG) rule: any new load over 75 MW — effectively every proposal on this map — must arrive with a matching generation or purchase plan rather than drawing on the shared grid. As of late August 2026 the AESO's draft Phase 2A implementation rules were also proposing that, in practice, only gas-fired generation (not wind or solar) would qualify to meet that requirement — a proposal still under review, reported by Canada's National Observer. This context is why so many proposals on this map pair a data centre with its own dedicated gas plant.

⚖️ Formally registering a concern with the Alberta Utilities Commission (AUC)

For projects that involve AUC review (e.g., proposed power-generation facilities tied to a data-centre project), the AUC accepts a Statement of Intent to Participate (SIP) from members of the public who want their concerns formally on the record for a specific proceeding. In general, the process works like this:

  1. Go to auc.ab.ca (a desktop/laptop browser is easier to use than a phone for this).
  2. Find "Access to the eFiling System" and create a new personal account, then confirm it via the email link the AUC sends you.
  3. Log in and continue to eFiling.
  4. Use the "Go To" search to find the proceeding number for the specific project you want to comment on (proceeding numbers are unique to each application — there is no single number that covers every project on this map; search the AUC's proceeding list or e‑Filing system for the project nearest you, or ask the AUC directly which proceeding applies).
  5. Click "Register" to participate, acknowledge the AUC's privacy policy, and select "Intervener" as your registration type.
  6. Fill in the Statement of Intent to Participate explaining your interest/concerns, and save to submit.

A Statement of Intent to Participate is generally used to explain why a proposed facility (e.g., a data centre and/or an associated power plant) raises concerns for you — for example, proximity to a community, agricultural land, water sources, or local infrastructure. Per the AUC's own guidance, you typically do not need to live within any specific buffer distance of a project to file a SIP.

This is a general summary of a public regulatory process — it is not legal advice, and the steps, screens, and proceeding numbers can change. Always confirm the current process and the correct proceeding number for your project of interest directly on auc.ab.ca before relying on it.

Glossary

MW (Megawatt)
A unit of power. 1 MW ≈ enough electricity to power roughly 750–1,000 average Alberta homes at once. Data centres are often described by their planned power draw in MW.
GWh (Gigawatt‑hour)
A unit of energy used over time (1 GWh = 1,000 MWh). Used here to compare a proposal's estimated annual electricity use to a community's.
dB (Decibel)
A measure of sound level. Roughly: 40 dB ≈ a quiet library, 55–65 dB ≈ a normal conversation/busy office, 65+ dB ≈ a vacuum cleaner up close. The sound demo on this map is a synthesized illustration, not a real recording.
m³/yr (Cubic metres per year)
A measure of water volume used over a year — used to compare a proposal's estimated water use to a community's residential use.
AUC (Alberta Utilities Commission)
The provincial regulator that reviews and approves major power-generation and transmission projects in Alberta, including on‑site generation proposed by some data centres.
EPEA
The Environmental Protection and Enhancement Act — Alberta's main environmental-approvals law; projects with significant emissions or water use may require an EPEA approval.
ASP (Area Structure Plan)
A municipal land-use planning document that guides how a parcel of land may be developed — often the stage where local councils and residents weigh in on a proposal.
Footprint
The physical land area (in km²) a proposal expects to occupy — shown on the map as a shaded shape so you can compare its size to your community.
"Not publicly disclosed"
Used throughout this map when a company or government body has not released a figure (e.g., investment amount, air-quality plan) as of the date shown — it does not imply one doesn't exist, only that it hasn't been published.

❔ Frequently asked questions

How big is Alberta's data centre boom overall?Alberta's total data centre capacity is projected to surge from 1.6 GW today to 13.2 GW — a more than 8× increase — if the proposals currently in planning and construction are all built out (Source: BUILDEX Alberta / Major Projects Alberta, June 2026). Separately, AESO's own grid-connection queue is larger still: as of February 2026 it held 42 large-load applications (most of them AI data centres) requesting a combined 21.1 GW — nearly twice Alberta's entire peak grid load today (CBC News, Feb. 2026). For context, Alberta's entire electricity grid currently supplies roughly 100,000 GWh/yr (about 12–15 GW peak). The 20 proposals tracked on this map are the subset of that queue with a named developer, a real site, and enough public reporting to compare responsibly.
Is this an official government or company map?No. This is an independent, informational comparison built from public announcements, news coverage, and government/regulatory filings. It is not affiliated with any of the companies, municipalities, or regulators shown.
Where do the numbers come from?Each figure is attributed to its source where possible (company announcements, municipal planning documents, regulatory filings, news reporting). Where no figure has been publicly released, it's marked "not publicly disclosed" rather than estimated or guessed.
Why do some proposals show "not publicly disclosed" for investment or air quality?Because, as of the date shown, no figure or plan for that item had been made public for that specific proposal. This map does not fill gaps with assumptions.
Is the "what it might sound like" demo a real recording?No — it's a synthesized illustration built to approximate the kind of low-frequency hum and broadband noise that large mechanical/cooling equipment can produce at different distances. It's offered as a rough sense of scale, not a measurement or prediction for any specific site.
Can I compare more than one proposal at a time?By design, no — figures from different proposals are shown one at a time and never combined or summed, since stacking unrelated projects' numbers together would be misleading. You can switch between proposals using the "Compare against" dropdown to see each on its own.
I think something here is inaccurate or out of date — how do I flag it?Use the "Contact" feature in the toolbar, or reach out via the contact details for the relevant regulator/municipality shown there. This map will be updated as new public information becomes available.
Is anyone tracking data centres outside Alberta too?Yes — in the U.S., reporter/activist Erin Brockovich launched a public "AI Data Center Reporting Map" (brockovichdatacenter.com, April 2026) where communities can report concerns; reporting on it has highlighted that many U.S. projects cluster in already drought-prone regions. The 💧 Water layer on this map now includes a note on which Alberta river basin each proposal sits in, including whether it's in the part of southern Alberta that's been closed to new water licences since 2006.
Why is Alberta specifically attracting so many data centres?Several factors combine to make Alberta unusually attractive: abundant and relatively affordable power (natural gas and renewables), a cold climate that reduces cooling costs year-round, the lowest corporate tax rate in Canada, and — critically — a dedicated AI Data Centre Concierge Program run by the provincial government that fast-tracks regulatory approvals and connects developers with land, power, and permitting resources. The flip side of that fast-track is that some projects have been approved with limited environmental review — which is exactly what this map is designed to help residents understand. Source: Government of Alberta; BUILDEX Alberta, June 2026.
Why isn't every Alberta AI data-centre proposal shown here?Because AESO's connection queue is much bigger than the 20 mapped here — 42 large-load applications requesting 21.1 GW combined, as of February 2026 (CBC News) — but most of the other ~22 are listed only by project code and megawatt figure, with no developer name, site, or footprint attached. Putting a project on this map means drawing it at a real location and a real size, so the bar for inclusion is: a named developer, a locatable site, and enough public reporting to compare it responsibly — the same "don't fill gaps with assumptions" rule the figures follow. Five projects cleared that bar since the last count: three near-identical Crusoe Energy / Kalina Distributed Power sites (Myers Energy Park in Red Deer County, Alsike Energy Park in Brazeau County, and Crossfield Energy Park in Rocky View County), Beacon Data Centers' sixth Alberta site in Chestermere, and the Indigenous-majority-owned Mihta Askiy Data Centre in Northern Sunrise County. As of August 2026, TransAlta's Keephills Data Centre (Parkland County, west of Edmonton — a Memorandum of Understanding with CPP Investments and Brookfield for an initial ~230 MW power-purchase agreement, potentially scaling to 1 GW, announced Feb. 2026) still hasn't published a site plan or footprint, so there's nothing yet to draw to scale; it remains a strong candidate once that information is public. The project formerly tracked here as "GLDC Load / Greenlight" has since been resolved: it's the Pembina–Kineticor–Morgan Stanley Greenlight Electricity Centre gas plant near Gibbons, which reached a final investment decision in 2026 specifically to power Meta's new Sturgeon Data Centre — now shown on this map in its own right. (Also worth noting: a roughly 1,100-acre Kineticor/eStruxture data-centre campus proposed for northeast Rocky View County was rejected 6–1 by county council in early 2026 after a lengthy public hearing, and Synapse's Olds data centre had its associated power plant rejected twice by the AUC — most recently Aug. 17, 2026 — with the company confirming the project "will not proceed as proposed." Both are different from, or discontinued versions of, the proposals tracked here.)
Rocky View County paused new data-centre applications — does that affect projects already on this map?No, not directly. On July 21, 2026, Rocky View County council voted 5–2 to stop accepting new applications to redesignate land for data-centre use (Bylaw C-8766-2026), and to reclassify data centres from a "permitted" to a "discretionary" use — giving the county more latitude to refuse permits — while it finishes a broader Emerging Sector Strategy expected this fall. Council was explicit that the moratorium does not cancel projects already moving through the pipeline, so Wild Rose Power Hub, Beacon AI Hub (Indus), and eStruxture CAL-3 — all in Rocky View County — continue through their existing approval processes. Source: CBC News, July–August 2026.
What is "BYOG" and why does it show up so often on this map?BYOG ("bring your own generation") is the province's core policy for connecting big new electricity users. Under the Data Centre Regulation (AR 117/2026, in force since June 9, 2026), any load over 75 MW — which covers essentially every proposal shown here — must arrive with its own matching generation (typically an on-site gas plant) or a firm purchase arrangement, rather than simply drawing on the shared grid. As of late August 2026, the AESO's draft detailed rules were also proposing that only gas-fired generation would qualify to meet that requirement, which critics (including Environmental Defence and the Pembina Institute) say locks out wind and solar for this sector even where developers would prefer it. This is why so many entries on this map are a data centre paired with its own dedicated gas power plant — it's now close to a regulatory requirement, not simply a business choice.
Does Alberta's foreign land-ownership law affect these projects?It can. Much of this land is "controlled land" under Alberta's Foreign Ownership of Land Regulations (FOLR), which generally caps a foreign citizen or foreign-controlled corporation at two parcels totalling under 20 acres; a narrower industrial-facility exemption allows up to 80 acres for a single facility. A data-centre campus is typically far larger than either limit, so a foreign-controlled developer needs a specific provincial order-in-council to acquire the land at all. In 2025, Alberta's Minister of Service Alberta put forward 13 such orders-in-council covering more than 10,600 acres province-wide; one approved October 29, 2025 let Beacon Data Centers acquire roughly 8.8 km² (~2,175 acres) for five data centres and associated electrical infrastructure, and a separate exemption the same day covered Vibro Information Services. This map doesn't audit the ownership structure or nationality of every proposal's investors — that would require a level of corporate-registry verification this project doesn't do. Where a parent company, joint-venture partner, or major backer is named on this map, it's because that fact was directly reported by the source cited alongside it and is material to understanding the deal (e.g., Meta's Greenlight power partner Pembina Pipeline, or Mihta Askiy's 51% Woodland Cree First Nation ownership) — not a general claim about who controls each company. Sources: MLT Aikins; Lawson Lundell; The Canadian Press (via Penticton Herald / Prince Albert Daily Herald), 2025–2026.
Is the public pushing back on any of this?Yes, significantly, especially through summer 2026. Hundreds protested Meta's Sturgeon County project at a Morinville, AB rally in early August; a man organized standalone protests to coincide with the province's own town halls; and the Alberta government's first two AI data-centre town halls (Lacombe and Redwater/Sturgeon County) drew loud, sometimes profane opposition from attendees over water, noise, electricity costs, and a lack of consultation. Alberta's NDP opposition launched its own province-wide "AI consultation tour," citing polling that found roughly 70% of Albertans oppose data-centre development in their own community (higher still in rural areas). Separately, Sturgeon Lake Cree Nation is in court arguing the province failed its duty to consult before granting Wonder Valley's water licence — a Court of King's Bench judge ruled in the Nation's favour on an early procedural question in August 2026, allowing that underlying legal challenge to proceed (it has not cancelled the licence or halted the project). Sources: CBC News, Canada's National Observer, The Globe and Mail, August 2026.
🎈 Fun fact — why does this map even exist?It started as a one-person curiosity project: someone wanted to see how the proposed Wonder Valley data centre would actually compare to the size of their own town — and was genuinely taken aback by how much bigger it looked once it was drawn to scale on a real map, rather than just read as a number on a page. That "wait, it's THAT big?" moment is the whole reason this tool exists — so anyone else can drop their own community onto the map and see the comparison for themselves, instead of just taking someone's word for it.
